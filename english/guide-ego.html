The Ego

Whether inflated or wounded, the ego turns meditation into the question 'Can I succeed?' The book asks you to see this constructed center.

Read the Book1. The Two Difficulties

What this chapter is saying

The book names ego as the first great obstacle on the way to meditation. From childhood a person is trained to compete, possess, and prove himself, gradually forming the boundary of 'mine—me—you—I.' Success inflates ego; failure wounds it. Either can turn meditation into another attempt to prove oneself.

The structure in the book

The book traces the formation of ego: first comes 'mine,' then 'me,' then 'you,' and finally 'I.' These identities have practical value in life but are not your true center. Later we gather other people's opinions, education, status, possessions, and roles and mistake the collection for ourselves.

What it means for practice

Meditation is not the construction of a more successful, spiritual, or special 'I.' As you move inward, old identities may loosen and even create a period of chaos and uncertainty; the book treats this as a transition, not a failure. The movement is from borrowed personality toward individuality.

See the Borrowed “Me”
  1. When the thought 'I should become...' appears, do not immediately obey it.
  2. Ask: where does this standard come from—family, school, society, comparison, or a real need now?
  3. Do not turn meditation into a score. Today, simply watch how ego creates stories of tension, success, and failure.
